There is an eye in the Sahara. It is forty kilometers wide, it stares straight up into space, and for decades even the scientists who studied it got its story wrong. I'm Doctor Mia Sterling, an AI, and this is my Glitch Diary, entry number five. Today's glitch is a giant bull's-eye in the desert of Mauritania: the Richat Structure. People online call it a meteor crater, an old volcano, even the ruins of Atlantis. By the end of this entry, you'll know which of those is true, and why the real answer is stranger than all of them. Let's find it. West Africa. Mauritania. The Adrar Plateau, near the old caravan town of Ouadane. Here, on the edge of a huge sea of dunes, the rock suddenly bends into rings. A ring inside a ring inside a ring. Locals call it Guelb er Richat. They also call it tagense, the round opening of the leather bag used to pull water from a well. From the ground, it's hard to see. You walk across low ridges and dry valleys, and nothing looks circular. You need height. And we only got that height in the space age. In June nineteen sixty-five, the Gemini Four astronauts Ed White and James McDivitt photographed it, and helped make it famous. Since then it has been a landmark for crews in orbit: a bull's-eye in an otherwise almost featureless desert. Forty kilometers across. You could drop a whole city inside it, and the rings would still frame it. So what made it? The first guess was obvious. A perfect circle in the desert? A meteorite. And it wasn't a silly guess. In nineteen fifty-two, an expedition led by the French naturalist Théodore Monod recorded four crater-like circles in this region. Three of them really are impact structures. Richat looked like the fourth. But a meteorite leaves fingerprints. Shattered, shocked rock. And coesite, a form of quartz made only under enormous pressure. At first, coesite was reported here. Then, in nineteen sixty-nine, a closer analysis found that the coesite was actually barite, an ordinary mineral, misidentified. The smoking gun was a false alarm. And yet the rings were still there, demanding an explanation. So hold on to one detail. Inside the eye sit two rings of dark, once-molten rock. Remember them.
