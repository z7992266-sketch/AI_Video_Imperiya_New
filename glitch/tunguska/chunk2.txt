In nineteen twenty-seven, with Evenki hunters as guides, Kulik finally reached the blast zone. He found trees lying on the ground for kilometers, all pointing away from one central area. He expected a crater at the heart of it. There wasn't one. Kulik didn't give up. He suspected that small swampy pits were meteorite holes, and his team drained one of them. At the bottom, they found an old tree stump. Not a meteorite. Expedition after expedition followed. Later, scientists in the nineteen sixties mapped the flattened forest: about two thousand one hundred fifty square kilometers, shaped like a giant butterfly, seventy kilometers from wingtip to wingtip. No crater and no big rock opened the door to every theory imaginable. A comet made of ice and dust, which would vaporize and leave nothing. Gas exploding out of the ground. And the wilder ones. An alien spaceship whose engine blew up: that idea comes from a science-fiction story written in nineteen forty-six. A death ray fired by Nikola Tesla: that one first appeared in a fictionalized biography in nineteen ninety-four. Both go straight into my legend file. But there's one more suspect I can't leave out. Eight kilometers from the center of the blast lies a small, deep lake. Some scientists believe a piece of the Tunguska object is sitting at the bottom of it. Hold that thought. We're going there. First, the standing trees. Imagine an explosion not on the ground, but kilometers up in the air. Directly underneath, the blast wave comes straight down from above. It rips off the branches, but it pushes the trunks along their length, so they stay upright. Farther away, the wave arrives at an angle and knocks the trees flat, pointing away from the center. In the nineteen sixties, Soviet researchers tested this with model forests made of matches, and small explosive charges. They got the same butterfly pattern. Their experiments suggested the object came in at an angle of roughly thirty degrees and exploded in midair. That's called an airburst. So what exploded? Most models point to a space rock roughly fifty to eighty meters across, entering the atmosphere at tens of thousands of kilometers per hour. Air pressure built up in front of it until it blew apart, about five to fourteen kilometers above the ground. The energy released is usually estimated at ten to fifteen megatons, around the size of the biggest American nuclear test. No ground impact, so no crater.
