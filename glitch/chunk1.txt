In the middle of one of the emptiest deserts on Earth, there is a hole in the ground that has been on fire for decades. It's about seventy meters wide, thirty meters deep, and at night you can see its glow from kilometers away. People call it the Door to Hell.

Almost every article will tell you the same thing: it was lit by Soviet engineers in 1971. But when scientists finally checked the satellite record, the story stopped adding up. And now that the flames are fading, the real problem turns out to be something you can't see at all.

This is Turkmenistan, in Central Asia. Most of the country is covered by the Karakum, the Black Sand desert. About two hundred and sixty kilometers north of the capital, Ashgabat, near a village called Darvaza, the sand suddenly opens.

Officially, the crater has a poetic name: the Shining of Karakum. Many others call it the Gates of Hell. And underneath it sits the reason this country matters: natural gas. Turkmenistan holds some of the largest gas reserves on the planet. So how did a gas field turn into a fire that wouldn't go out?

The version you'll find almost everywhere goes like this. In 1971, Soviet engineers were drilling here. Their rig hit a cavern full of natural gas. The ground gave way, and the equipment fell into the collapse.

Now they had a giant hole leaking gas into the air, gas that can harm people and animals nearby. So, the story goes, they made a simple decision: set it on fire, burn the gas off, and wait a few weeks. It's a perfect story. Maybe too perfect.

In 2013, explorer George Kourounis prepared to climb into the crater, and he asked local Turkmen geologists what really happened. Their answer was different. According to them, the crater collapsed in the 1960s, and it was only set on fire in the 1980s.

Nobody could prove either version. The original Soviet records are missing, classified, or simply inaccessible. Even Kourounis admitted: I don't know exactly what happened.

Then, recently, a team of researchers did something clever. They went back through decades of Landsat satellite images, and looked for the first sign of fire. The glow appears between late 1987 and early 1988. Not 1971. The famous story was off by almost seventeen years.
