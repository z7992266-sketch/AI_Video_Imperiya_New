Back to 2013. After about a year and a half of preparation, Kourounis became the first person known to set foot on the bottom of the crater. He wore a heat-reflective suit, carried his own air supply, and hung from heat-resistant ropes.

He described the sound as a jet engine, a roaring, high-pressure, gas-burning sound. And the view, as a coliseum of fire, with thousands of small flames everywhere you look. But he wasn't there for the view. He was there to collect soil.

In that soil, scientists found something unexpected: bacteria, living at the bottom of a burning pit. Bacteria that weren't found in the soil around the crater. Life, in a place that looks like it shouldn't have any.

Here's something most people don't expect: you can actually visit. In 2013 the area was declared a nature reserve, and travelers drive for hours across the desert on a rough track to camp in yurts near the rim. There are no railings and no ticket booth. Just sand, wind, and the sound of the fire.

Visitors describe a strange feeling at the edge: a wave of heat on your face, while your back is cold in the desert night. The best time to see it is after sunset, when the glow lights up the smoke above the pit.

For decades the fire was a strange local landmark. Then it became a national problem. In 2010, Turkmenistan's president called for measures to limit the crater's impact on nearby gas fields. In January 2022, he went further, and ordered experts to find a way to extinguish it, citing people's health, the environment, and valuable gas simply burning away.
