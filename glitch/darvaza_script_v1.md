## SCRIPT

### [0:00] COLD OPEN
*(Night. Roaring flames. Slow push toward the rim. No music for 3 seconds — only the fire.)*

In the middle of one of the emptiest deserts on Earth, there is a hole in the ground that has been on fire for more than fifty years.

It's about seventy metres wide. Thirty metres deep. And at night, you can see its glow from kilometres away.

People call it the Door to Hell.

But the strangest thing about this place isn't the fire. It's that nobody can tell you — with certainty — how it started. And now that the flames are finally dying, scientists are worried about something you can't see at all.

### [0:40] CHAPTER 1 — Where is this?
*(3D map: zoom from Central Asia into Turkmenistan, then the Karakum desert, a red pin at Darvaza.)*

This is Turkmenistan, in Central Asia. Most of the country is covered by the Karakum — the "black sand" desert. About two hundred and sixty kilometres north of the capital, Ashgabat, near a village called Darvaza, the sand suddenly opens.

Officially, the crater has a poetic name: the "Shining of Karakum." Everyone else calls it the Gates of Hell.

And underneath it sits one of the reasons this country matters to the world: natural gas. Turkmenistan holds some of the largest gas reserves on the planet.

So how did a gas field turn into a fire that wouldn't go out?

### [1:40] CHAPTER 2 — The story everyone tells
*(Archival-style recreation: desert, drilling rig silhouettes, map animation — clearly illustrative, no fake "archive footage" labels.)*

The version you'll find almost everywhere goes like this.

In 1971, Soviet engineers were drilling here, searching for oil. Their rig hit a cavern full of natural gas. The ground gave way. The rig and the camp fell into the collapse.

Now they had a problem: a giant hole leaking gas into the air — gas that can poison people and animals nearby.

So they made a decision that sounds simple: set it on fire. Burn the gas off. It should take a few weeks.

That was more than fifty years ago.

It's a perfect story. Maybe too perfect.

### [3:00] CHAPTER 3 — The glitch in the story
*(Text on screen: "1971?" then "1960s?" then "1980s?" glitching between each other.)*

When explorer George Kourounis prepared to climb into the crater in 2013, he asked local Turkmen geologists what really happened.

Their answer was different. According to them, the crater collapsed in the **1960s** — and stayed unlit for years. It was set on fire only in the **1980s**.

And here's the part that turns this into a real mystery: nobody has been able to find the original records. Researchers who went looking found that the documents are missing, classified, or simply inaccessible.

So we have a hole that has been burning for decades — and two different stories about when, and why, it was lit.

Kourounis himself put it honestly: *"I don't know exactly what happened."*

### [4:20] CHAPTER 4 — The man who went inside
*(Map of the crater rim, a dotted line descending. Photos only where licensed.)*

In November 2013, after about a year and a half of preparation, Kourounis became the first person known to set foot on the bottom of the crater.

He wore a heat-reflective suit, carried his own air supply, and hung from special heat-resistant ropes.

He described the sound as *"like a jet engine — this roaring, high-pressure, gas-burning sound."* And the view: *"a coliseum of fire — everywhere you look, thousands of these small fires."*

He wasn't there for the thrill. He collected soil samples.

And in that soil, scientists found something unexpected: **bacteria** — living at the bottom of a burning pit. Bacteria that weren't found in the soil around the crater.

Life, in the one place on Earth that looks like it shouldn't have any.

### [5:40] CHAPTER 5 — "Put it out"
*(News-style typography: 2010 → 2022.)*

For decades, the fire was a strange local landmark. Then it became a national problem.

In 2010, Turkmenistan's president called for measures to limit the crater's impact on nearby gas development.

In January 2022, he went further: he announced the crater should be extinguished — citing people's health, the environment, and the value of the gas simply burning away. A commission was created to find a way.

But how do you turn off a fire that is fed from underground?

### [6:40] CHAPTER 6 — It's working… and that's the problem
*(Graph: "flame intensity" line going down; second line "methane" — labelled with sources.)*

In June 2025, specialists from the state gas company said the fire had been reduced about **three times**. They had drilled wells nearby to pull the gas away before it reaches the crater.

Independent analysis of satellite data later suggested the flame's heat had dropped by more than seventy-five percent in three years.

So the Door to Hell is closing. Good news, right?

Here's the twist.

Fire actually does something useful here: it turns methane into carbon dioxide. Both warm the planet — but methane is far stronger, around eighty times more powerful than CO₂ over twenty years.

And satellites from the Carbon Mapper project have measured methane escaping from the crater area — on average about **thirteen hundred kilograms every hour** between 2022 and 2025.

That means the real danger was never the flames you can see. It's the gas you can't.

If the gas is captured and used — the crater's fade is a win. If it simply leaks out unburned — a darker crater could actually be worse for the climate.

### [8:20] ENDING
*(Back to the rim at night. Slow pull-out to the 3D desert map, the glow shrinking to a dot.)*

For half a century, this hole has been a glitch in the desert: a fire nobody planned, a history nobody wrote down, and life where life shouldn't exist.

Soon, the light may go out completely.

So here's my question for you: should the Door to Hell be put out for good — or kept burning as a monument?

Tell me in the comments.

I'm Mia. This was GLITCHVERSE. Real places. Real glitches.
