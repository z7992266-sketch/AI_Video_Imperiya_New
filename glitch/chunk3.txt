In June 2025, specialists from the state gas company said the fire had been reduced about three times. They had drilled wells nearby to pull gas away before it reaches the crater. Satellite data agree: the flames have been getting weaker.

So the Door to Hell is closing. Good news, right? Here's the twist. That same satellite study used five different hyperspectral satellites, and it found methane escaping from the crater, roughly one to three tonnes every hour, between 2020 and 2025. And while the flames got weaker, the methane showed no clear decline.

That matters, because fire turns methane into carbon dioxide. Both warm the planet, but methane is far stronger: around eighty times more powerful than carbon dioxide over twenty years. So if the gas is captured and used, a darker crater is a win. If it simply leaks out unburned, a darker crater could be worse.

And Darvaza isn't the only place on Earth where the ground has been burning for decades. In the town of Centralia, Pennsylvania, a coal mine fire started in 1962, and it is still burning underground. Almost the entire town was abandoned, and smoke still rises from cracks in the ground.

Near Baku, in Azerbaijan, a hillside called Yanar Dag, the burning mountain, has natural gas flames dancing along the rock. Near Kirkuk in Iraq, the eternal fire of Baba Gurgur is said to have burned for thousands of years. And on the coast of Turkey, the flames of the Chimaera have been escaping from the mountain rocks for thousands of years. Ancient sailors are said to have used them like a lighthouse.

What makes Darvaza different is the mix of everything: a giant open pit, a missing history, a satellite record that rewrote the story, and a fire that might be doing the planet a favor by burning.

For almost forty years, this hole has been a glitch in the desert: a fire nobody can fully explain, a history nobody wrote down, and life where life shouldn't exist.
