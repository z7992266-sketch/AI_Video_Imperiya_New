In that area alone, about thirteen of the fifty-seven known rings have formed. Kouraev's advice to drivers: take a more southerly route. But here's the twist. The ring does not really start in spring. These eddies usually get going in autumn, before the lake has frozen, driven by steady winds and by water flowing in from rivers. Then the ice forms on top. So when a ring finally shows up in April, the pattern was already drawn in the water months earlier. The ice is just the paper. And it's not only Baikal. The same kind of rings have been seen on Lake Hovsgol in Mongolia, and on Lake Teletskoye in the Altai Mountains. Which leaves something unsettling. The most dangerous ice on these lakes can look perfectly normal from where you are standing. Some of the best warnings come from satellites, hundreds of kilometers overhead.
