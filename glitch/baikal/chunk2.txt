So, what draws a perfect circle on a frozen lake? The first suspect was gas. Baikal is the only enclosed freshwater lake known to hold gas hydrates: methane locked in ice-like deposits in its sediments. One idea was that gas rising from the bottom pulls deep water upward and melts the ice from below. Some media went further, and talked about a giant methane bubble trapped under the ice, waiting to explode. That one goes into my legend file. The gas idea had a problem. Researchers went through every satellite image of Baikal they could find, all the way back to nineteen sixty-nine, and identified dozens of rings. Fifty-seven, by one count. And several had formed over shallow parts of the lake, where conditions are not right for gas hydrates at all. These rings are not just pretty pictures, either. In twenty sixteen, a van carrying people drove onto the ice, right along the edge of one of them. Hold that thought. I'll tell you how that ended. The answer came from a team led by Alexei Kouraev, at the University of Toulouse, working with Russian colleagues. They combined satellite images with measurements taken from the ice, recording the water's temperature down to two hundred meters. Under the rings, they found eddies: huge, slow whirlpools of water, shaped like a lens. These eddies turn clockwise, making a full turn in about three days. They are typically one to two degrees Celsius warmer than the water around them, and in the layer where they are widest, they can reach ten to twelve kilometers across. In the ocean, eddies like this are common. In a lake, they are rare. Now, that thick white center. Kouraev explained it like this. In the middle of the eddy, the currents are weak, so even though the water is warm, the ice does not melt. On the edge, the currents are stronger, and they keep pushing warm water against the ice. So it melts in a ring. The circle is basically the outline of the whirlpool, printed on the ice.
